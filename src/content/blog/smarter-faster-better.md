Book review time.

Smarter, Faster, Better by Charles Duhigg.

Sounds like a Daft Punk song.

I like the book. It is a pretty typical business book. Studies or other evidence surrounded by anecdotes of how the principles applied to certain groups in specific situations.

I read this book because I’m again a manager after several years, so I wanted to refresh my skills.

His first book, *The Power of Habit*, is better known. I read it and found it interesting, but hard to apply to myself. Then along came Atomic Habits, which is full of actionable advice and made Power of Habit seem underpowered.

So it is with this book. Lots of really good points, and how they applied to different groups. For example, he explains how Disney used creative brokers (the Pixar people) and creative desperation to push the movie "Frozen" across the finish line.

But compared to books such as *The Manager’s Path*, it seems abstract. Perhaps that is good, as it allows the reader to think about how to apply the principles to their own situations.

## **8 Principles**

There are 8 principles to becoming smarter, faster, and better.

- Motivation - giving a locus of control and a bias towards action creates motivation

- Psychological Safety (Teams) - how teams that provide psychological safety help themselves have greater productivity

- Focus - mental models that help you envision what will happen and what could get in your way of getting there

- Goal Setting - SMART and stretch

- Managing Others - push decisions down to the people who know the best

- Decision Making - consider multiple possibilities and use Bayesian thinking to sort through them

- Innovation - creativity can come from desperation and shaking things up

- Absorbing Data - you need to have a hands-on approach to working with data

Each of these is accompanied by one or more stories that illustrates the principle. The stories are interesting and do a good job illustrating the principle.

But again the principles aren’t “do this”. If that’s your preference, then this should be right for you.

This is not to say that the advice is not practical, but it will take the reader some perspective and reflection to make use of it.

The principles aren’t independent. The book doesn't organize them this way, but this is how they fit together in my head:

**Motivation → Goals → Focus → Decisions → Action**

with psychological safety, delegation, innovation, and data operating around that core loop. (Action isn’t one of the principles, but all of these lead to action.)

So, how am I going to use what I read?

## Appendix - A Reader’s Guide To Using These Ideas

Mr. Duhigg does leave some crumbs for the reader though. The appendix is the real gem of the book. He talks about how he applied most of the principles while he was writing the book.

As someone who would like to write a book themselves someday, I found this part the most helpful.

### Motivation

I don’t know if he meant this or not, but motivation seems to be the root principle. Without it, pursuing the others doesn’t seem possible.

- Motivation becomes easier when we transform a chore into a choice. Doing so gives a sense of control.

The main advice point is to “just start”. To write his emails, he would just write the first sentence, unfiltered for each email. Once that was done, he could go back and write the actual message, but starting with the seed gave him that motivation.

For myself, being a software engineer, I have quite a few meetings and maintenance tasks that need to be done. I don’t find these particularly satisfying.

To get beyond this, I frequently select a lightweight task to perform during the meeting, something that doesn’t require deep concentration. The idea is that I can still be productive while listening to items that aren’t related to my own work, but can still be present enough to answer questions when they are related. (Not all meetings are this way. Some are pertinent to my work, but you know the types of meetings I’m talking about.)

For other items, I schedule particular times of the day to do them (akin to time blocking), and otherwise do them early in the day (eating the frog) or put them off so I don’t think about them while I’m doing the “real work” that needs to be done

If I’m going through a real slump, I’ll use Pomodoros and time tracking to help me focus.

### Psychological Safety

This isn’t something you give yourself, but something you deliver to others. As a manager of a small team (very small), it would seem that it wouldn’t be that hard. Of course, to know if I do engender a feeling of safety, you’d have to ask my team.

The main principle is just making sure that everyone is heard and feels like they have equal weight in the decisions. Manage the *how*, not *who*.

It’s not hard to make sure everyone is heard, but it can be difficult to make decisions carry equal weight. Sometimes some suggestions just aren’t feasible. But you can let everyone know their contributions are appreciated, with specific examples.

Reading Dale Carnegie’s *How to Win Friends and Influence People* is probably the best advice I can give for this principle.

### Goals

There is so much literature on goal setting that I will refrain from making any suggestions other than the bare minimum.

Goals are pretty straightforward. Make a stretch goal that is just out of your comfort zone, and then make the sub-goals S.M.A.R.T. Try to make the sub-goals so that even if you don’t reach the stretch goal, you’ve accomplished something that makes you happy.

My own stretch goal is to become a better manager. Reading books like this, deliberately changing how I run meetings, and pushing more decisions down to my developers (see below) are concrete sub-goals toward that.

### Focus  

The way to gain focus is to *plan* for it. Imagine the future interruptions possible and plan for those. 

Normally you get advice such as turn off your phone, write down thoughts that would otherwise interrupt your flow, but creating a plan to deal with distractions and interruptions is what the author does.

As a sample of something I might do:

- I need to create a design document for a feature, here is my plan

This one is particularly difficult in the age of AI agents. You want to keep them busy, but doing so means jumping between agents to set them on their next task.

Focus is required to help you reach your goals, so incorporating your goals into the above plans is good.

### Decision Making

To make goals and maintain focus requires making decisions. It seems so easy. Take inventory of available choices, and then pick one. But which one is best?

This is where “bias to action” in motivation can become handy. Make a decision that you think will move you forward.

Bayesian thinking means starting with what you currently believe, making the best decision you can from that information, and then updating your beliefs as new evidence arrives.

And if you’re paralyzed by which choice to make, just choose something. (If no one comes from the future to stop you, then how bad of a decision can it really be?)

### Managing Others

As a new (again) manager, this is why I picked up this book. The main advice is to trust your people and to use agile techniques whenever possible. 

Your reports know what they’re doing, and trusting them to make the correct decisions has all sorts of benefits (velocity, psychological safety, etc.)

What I should ask myself… What decision am I currently making that one of my developers should be making instead?

### Encourage Innovation

Creativity comes from combining ideas in new ways. Set up an environment where this is possible.

Ideas can only come from our own experiences. Involving multiple people increases the experiences available for ideas.

Be careful though that one train of creativity doesn’t shut off other tracks.

How could I deliberately mix ideas or people in my actual work? (There is a really good [article by Lalit Maganti](https://lalitm.com/post/find-problems-staff-engineer/) that gives a good description of how this might look.)

### Absorbing Data

What does it mean to “absorb data”? To absorb something is to make it part of you. More than just looking at it, but to integrate it.

In the story that he presents about the Cincinnati school that improved their test scores through data, it was the active handling of the data, the copying, sorting, and compiling, that really helped the teachers see what needed to be done for each student.

So, using the data, is really the heart of this improvement. But to use it, I guess you need to absorb it first, which is what this principle is really about.

This first assumes that you have the data to absorb and use. And that can be the most difficult part, determining where to get the data and making it available. But if you have data, and really want to make use of it, then “getting your hands dirty” with it really is the best way to make it useful.

What data at work am I currently consuming as a dashboard that I might learn more from by playing with it?

## Conclusion

As mentioned above, I liked the book. I feel it’s a good survey of the field of productivity. And that’s the most interesting part, it’s up to the reader to decipher how to put the principles into practice.

And that is the crux of this review, that writing it forced me to do exactly that. I understood the book better once I stopped simply reading its ideas and started rearranging them, connecting them, and deciding how I would use them.

That’s how I’m going to use it: not as a system, but as a set of principles to revisit.
